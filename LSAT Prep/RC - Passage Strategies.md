# RC — Passage Strategies

> RC rewards structure, not speed-reading. You're not reading to absorb — you're reading to map.
> Back to [[LSAT Prep Hub]]

---

## The Four Passage Types

1. **Law / Policy** — debates about legislation, court decisions, legal theory
2. **Science / Natural World** — biology, physics, environmental science, medicine
3. **Humanities / Arts** — literature, music, history, philosophy
4. **Social Science** — economics, psychology, sociology, anthropology

Each type has tendencies. Science passages often present a hypothesis and debate its merits. Humanities passages often contrast two interpretations of a work or figure. Know the genre — it helps you anticipate structure.

---

## The Active Reading Method

### Step 1: Read the First Sentence of Each Paragraph Carefully
The first sentence almost always signals what the paragraph *does* (introduces a claim, presents a counterargument, gives evidence, offers a resolution).

### Step 2: Assign Each Paragraph a Purpose Label
As you finish each paragraph, write a 3–5 word label in the margin (or in your head):
- "introduces main claim"
- "presents opposing view"
- "author rebuts opposing view"
- "gives example/evidence"
- "qualifies the claim"

### Step 3: Track Three Things Throughout
1. **Main Point** — What is the passage *ultimately* arguing or explaining?
2. **Author's Perspective / Tone** — Does the author endorse, criticize, or stay neutral? What words reveal this?
3. **Structure** — How does the argument build? What's the logical relationship between paragraphs?

### Step 4: Don't Memorize Details — Know *Where* They Are
You will almost never need to remember a specific detail. You need to know which paragraph it's in so you can go back.

## Identifying the Main Point

Ask: "If I had to summarize this passage in one sentence to a friend, what would I say?"

It should:
- Cover the *whole* passage (not just one paragraph)
- Reflect the author's view (not just present facts)
- Be specific (not so broad it could apply to anything)

---

## Reading for Author's Tone

Watch for **evaluative language** — words that reveal the author's stance:
- Positive: *innovative, crucial, compelling, important, effectively*
- Negative: *flawed, problematic, misguided, fails to, overlooks*
- Neutral/Hedging: *suggests, may, could, appears to, some argue*

The LSAT often asks about the author's attitude. Nail this while reading, not after.

## Common Passage Structures

### Thesis–Evidence
Author makes a claim → supports it with evidence.
*What to track:* The claim and whether each paragraph is evidence for it.

### Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis
Presents a view → challenges it → offers a resolution or new perspective.
*What to track:* Where the author lands at the end.

### Phenomenon–Explanation
Presents a puzzling fact or trend → offers explanations.
*What to track:* How many explanations, which the author favors, and why.

### Two Perspectives
Presents two competing views (often on the same text or event).
*What to track:* What each side argues and which (if any) the author endorses.

## Timing Strategy

- **Aim:** ~8–9 minutes per passage set (passage + questions)
- Read the passage first, then all questions — don't jump back and forth mid-passage
- For difficult passages: spend more time mapping the structure, less time trying to understand every sentence
