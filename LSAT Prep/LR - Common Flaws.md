# LR — Common Flaws

> The LSAT recycles the same ~12 flaws over and over. Learn to recognize them on sight.
> Back to [[LSAT Prep Hub]]

---

## The Big Flaws

### 1. Correlation ≠ Causation
**What it is:** The argument assumes that because two things happen together (or in sequence), one *caused* the other.
**Example:** "Crime rates fell after the new policy. Therefore, the policy reduced crime."
**Why it fails:** Both could be caused by a third factor; the order could be reversed; it could be coincidence.
**Answer language:** "fails to consider that both X and Y might be caused by a third factor"

---

### 2. Unrepresentative Sample / Hasty Generalization
**What it is:** Drawing a broad conclusion from a small, biased, or unrepresentative sample.
**Example:** "We surveyed 50 gym-goers and found they're all healthy. Therefore, exercise makes people healthy."
**Why it fails:** The sample (gym-goers) is not representative of the general population.
**Answer language:** "assumes the sample is representative of the larger population"

---

### 3. Equivocation / Term Shift
**What it is:** A key word is used in two different senses in the same argument.
**Example:** "The law requires a *fair* trial. A coin flip is *fair*. Therefore, a coin flip satisfies the law."
**Why it fails:** "Fair" means something different in each use.
**Answer language:** "uses the term X in two different senses"

---

### 4. Appeal to Authority
**What it is:** Using an authority's opinion as proof without establishing the authority is relevant or credible.
**Example:** "This famous actor says the medication works. So it must work."
**Why it fails:** The authority has no relevant expertise.

---

### 5. Ad Hominem (Attacking the Person)
**What it is:** Dismissing an argument by attacking the person making it rather than the argument itself.
**Example:** "You can't trust his climate research — he drives an SUV."

---

### 6. Circular Reasoning
**What it is:** The conclusion is just a restatement of a premise — the argument assumes what it's trying to prove.
**Example:** "This book is reliable because it says so, and we know it says so because it's reliable."

---

### 7. False Dilemma / Either-Or
**What it is:** Assumes there are only two options when others exist.
**Example:** "Either we cut the budget or we raise taxes. We won't cut the budget. Therefore, we must raise taxes."
**Why it fails:** Ignores other possibilities (borrowing, reducing waste, etc.).
**Answer language:** "overlooks the possibility that there are other alternatives"

---

### 8. Composition / Division
**What it is:**
- **Composition:** What's true of the parts must be true of the whole.
- **Division:** What's true of the whole must be true of the parts.
**Example (Composition):** "Each player on the team is talented. Therefore, the team is talented."

---

### 9. Appeal to Popularity
**What it is:** Assumes something is true or good because many people believe or do it.
**Example:** "Most people think the policy is fair, so it must be."

---

### 10. Absence of Evidence
**What it is:** Assumes that because something hasn't been proven, it's false (or vice versa).
**Example:** "No study has shown that X causes Y. Therefore, X doesn't cause Y."
**Answer language:** "mistakes a lack of evidence for evidence of absence"

---

### 11. Scope Shift / Percentage vs. Absolute Numbers
**What it is:** Confuses percentages with raw numbers, or shifts between group sizes.
**Example:** "A higher *percentage* of women voted. Therefore, more women voted." (False if far fewer women were eligible.)

---

### 12. Necessary vs. Sufficient Confusion
**What it is:** Treats something that is sufficient as if it is necessary, or vice versa.
**Example:** "All murderers are violent. John is violent. Therefore, John is a murderer."
**Why it fails:** Being violent is not *sufficient* to be a murderer — it's not even necessary.
> See [[LR - Conditional Logic]] for a deep dive on this.

---

## Flaw Answer Choice Language Cheat Sheet

| The argument... | Means... |
|----------------|----------|
| "...fails to consider that..." | It ignores an alternative explanation |
| "...assumes without justification that..." | It treats something as proven that isn't |
| "...takes for granted that..." | Same as above |
| "...mistakes X for Y" | Confuses two different things |
| "...overlooks the possibility that..." | Ignores alternatives |
| "...draws a conclusion that goes beyond what the evidence supports" | Hasty generalization or overly strong claim |
