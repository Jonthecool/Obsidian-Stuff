## 📘 The Complete LSAT Guide — LSATwithJack

**Study Habits**
- Study 1–2 hours/day max. When it's up, stop and move on. Don't let LSAT consume your life.
- No music while studying. Test day has no music — don't get used to it.
- Always answer every question. A guess is worth more than a blank.
- Don't set a test date prematurely. Study until you hit your target score, *then* book.
- Keep at least 2 weeks of your schedule planned ahead at all times — removes daily decision fatigue.
- Run your own race. Don't compare your score to others.

**Blind Review Method**
- Mark uncertain questions *during* the test.
- After finishing, review all marked questions *before* checking answers — change any you now think are wrong.
- Review both your original and post-blind-review score separately.

**Argument Structure**
- Every argument has three parts: **Context** (background, often irrelevant), **Premises** (evidence), **Conclusion** (main point).
- Context is often fluff — ignore it, focus on premises + conclusion.
- Always accept premises as true. Never accept the conclusion automatically.
- Conclusion indicators: *therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently, accordingly, as a result, it follows that, implies that*
- Premise indicators: *since, for, given that, seeing as, as indicated by*

**Conditional Logic**
- Every conditional statement can be rewritten as "If X → Y."
- Contrapositive (~Y → ~X) is always valid.
- Converse (Y → X) is always **invalid** — this is the most common trap.
- Negation = logical opposite. "Not good" ≠ "bad." Negation of good = "not good."
- In a logic chain, if you have to reverse direction *without* negating, the relationship is invalid.

**LSAT Language**
- **OR** on the LSAT is inclusive — means one, the other, or both — unless "but not both" is specified.
- **SOME** = more than 0%, up to 100%.
- **MOST** = more than 50%, up to 100%.
- Negation of SOME = None (0%). Negation of MOST = 50% or less. Negation of ALL = Not all (less than 100%).

## 📙 The Loophole in LSAT Logical Reasoning — Ellen Cassidy

**Translation (Core Skill)**
- Translate *every* stimulus into your own words before answering anything. This is not optional.
- Translation is not "getting the gist." It's complete comprehension. If you can't retell it, you don't understand it.
- Cover the stimulus with a Post-It after reading — if you can't retell it from memory, go back.
- Translating turns the stimulus into a story you own. You're far more likely to retain a story than a passage.
- The LSAT writes deliberately difficult sentences. Difficulty = intentional. Translation is the countermeasure.
- Use commas to break complex sentences into manageable pieces.

**Power Players**
- **Must / Cannot** = 100% certainty (the 100% and the 0%)
- **Could / Not necessarily** = possibility (the 1–100% and the 0–99%)
- The Power Player in the conclusion determines the logical bar you must clear. A "could" conclusion is much easier to prove than a "must" conclusion.
- Hedging words (may, might, could, some, possible) *lower* the bar — they make conclusions easier to prove. This is why hedged answer choices often win.

**The Loophole (Gap in Argument)**
- The Loophole = the gap between premises and conclusion. The argument assumes something it never states.
- Four types of Loopholes:
  1. **Dangling Variable** — a new term appears in the conclusion that wasn't in the premises
  2. **Secret Value Judgments** — the argument assumes something is good/bad without proving it
  3. **Secret Downsides** — the argument ignores negative consequences of its conclusion
  4. **Assumed Universal Goals** — assumes everyone shares the same goal without establishing it

**Powerful vs. Provable Questions**
- **Powerful Questions** (Weaken, Strengthen, SA, Counter, Contradiction, Evaluate, Resolution) — you bring in new information.
- **Provable Questions** (Conclusion, Inference, MSS, Fill-In, Controversy, Agreement, NA, Argument Part, Flaw) — the answer must come directly from the stimulus.
- Know which type you're dealing with *before* reading answer choices. The approach is completely different.

**Answer Choice Strategy**
- Read *all* answer choices all the way through before selecting. Never stop at the first one that sounds good.
- Know what you want *before* reading answer choices. Confidence against temptation.
- **Red Flag answer choices:** "best way," "important," crazy nonsense, grouped extreme, opposite claim, dormant conditionals, comparatives/absolutes that aren't in the stimulus.
- **Strong Powerful answers:** powerful conditionals, grouped opposites.
- **Strong Provable answers:** provable conditionals, weak/hedged language.
- A Bias and E Bias: be aware that you can be subconsciously drawn to A or E answers. Reset between questions.

**Wrong Answer Journal**
- Keep a Wrong Answer Journal — log every question you miss, why you picked your answer, and what actually went wrong.
- Review your Wrong Answer Journal *before* each study session to stay calibrated.
- Ask two questions for every wrong answer: (1) What did I misunderstand in the stimulus? (2) What made me pick the wrong answer choice?

**General**
- The answer is always in the stimulus. Never go outside it.
- Question the author's claims. Don't just say "sounds good." Get critical.
- Read intently — every word matters. The LSAT is the "incorruptible skimming police."

## 📗 The LSAT Trainer — Mike Kim

**Core Mindset**
- The LSAT is a *skills and habits* test, not a knowledge test. Skills determine your ceiling; habits determine whether you reach it.
- Habits are formed early and are very hard to change. Start with correct habits from day one — bad habits formed in week 1 persist to test day.
- Learn by multiplication, not addition. Connect ideas to what you already know rather than memorizing disconnected lists. Compounding understanding beats accumulation.
- Natural aptitude matters less than you think. Consistent effort and correct habits are the dominant factor.

**The Only Flaw That Matters**
- The LSAT only cares about one type of flaw: the relationship between the support and the conclusion. Not whether the premises are true. Not whether the conclusion is true.
- Your job: take the premises as true, then ask whether they *absolutely prove* the conclusion. If not, there's a flaw.
- Never evaluate the truth of premises. Never evaluate the truth of the conclusion. Only evaluate whether the support justifies the conclusion.

**Common Flaw — Apples ≠ Oranges (False Equivalence)**
- The most common flaw: the argument falsely equates two different things. It treats X and Y as interchangeable when they're not.
- Example: "The average salary is $17,000, so most employees must make over $17,000." Average ≠ majority. The average can be high even if most employees earn very little, due to outliers.
- This flaw is easy to miss when subjects *seem* similar. Slow down and check whether the two things are actually equivalent.

**Answering Questions**
- Expect a high level of certainty before selecting an answer. Know *at least one absolute reason* why each wrong answer is wrong before committing to the right one.
- The most attractive wrong answer on Required Assumption questions often strengthens the argument — but isn't actually required for it to work.
- Think about *what kind* of thing the right answer needs to be before reading the choices. Predicting the answer type protects you from attractive wrong answers.
- Getting a question right requires many skills — a wrong turn at any step leads to the wrong answer. Build a reliable process, not just knowledge.

**Flaw as Foundation**
- Getting good at identifying flaws is the primary skill. Once you see flaws clearly, Weaken, Strengthen, and Assumption questions all become easier — they all require understanding why the support doesn't justify the conclusion.
- Flaw identification feeds at least four question types. It's the highest-leverage skill to develop first.
